Population Density Analysis in ArcGIS Pro: Methods and Applications in Urban Planning

Urban GIS Lab · November 7, 2026

A district can hold many residents and still be relatively low density if they occupy a wide area. A smaller population on a tight footprint can produce a higher density. Population counts, density values, and density maps answer different questions.

This article explains population density analysis in ArcGIS Pro at a conceptual level. It is not a click-by-click workflow. The Udemy course Urban Spatial Analysis & Modeling with ArcGIS Pro includes the applied lectures.

What Is Population Density Analysis?

A population count answers “how many people?” Population density answers “how many people relative to the land they occupy?”

Population density = Total population / Land area

If a district has 48,000 residents on 6 km², density is 8,000 people per km². The same 48,000 residents on 16 km² produce 3,000 people per km². The count is unchanged. The relationship between people and land is not.

  • Population count is the number of people in a unit.
  • Population density is that number divided by an area.
  • Density visualization is the graphic display of the ratio.

Coloring a map by the count field shows where large populations are. It is not yet a density map.

Why Population Density Matters in Urban Planning

School demand, open space, transit load, and emergency planning depend on how people are arranged, not only on a citywide total. Read beside building coverage, floor area, and land use, density helps show whether many residents come from compact buildings, crowded dwellings, or a large area of modest buildings. It does not describe income, age, or housing quality.

Understanding Different Spatial Scales

The same residents, grouped into different boundaries, produce different densities. That is the practical meaning of the modifiable areal unit problem.

Parcel level

Parcel population is often estimated by apportioning census counts with floor area or dwelling counts. A tower on a small lot can show very high parcel density.

Neighborhood level

Neighborhoods show local contrasts. A boundary that includes a large park looks less dense than the same housing on residential land only.

District level

Districts support citywide comparison. One moderate value can hide both a high-rise cluster and a sparse edge.

Gross density = Population / Total land area

Net residential density = Population / Residential land area

Example: 12,000 residents, 150 ha total, 60 ha residential → gross density 80 people/ha, net 200 people/ha. Both are correct. They describe different land bases.

A precise-looking density value still depends on the zone, the population data, and the way area was measured.

Population Density Mapping Using GIS

  1. Define the unit: parcel, neighborhood, district, or a regular grid.
  2. Attach a population count.
  3. Measure the denominator and record whether it is gross or net.
  4. Divide population by area.
  5. Symbolize the ratio, not the count.

ArcGIS Pro can calculate geometry and classify results. It does not decide whether the unit is appropriate.

Related Spatial Analysis Techniques

Building Coverage Ratio

BCR = Building footprint area / Parcel area

BCR describes ground coverage, not residents.

Floor Area Ratio

FAR = Total floor area / Parcel area

FAR captures vertical intensity. It is a built-form measure, not a demographic one.

Kernel Density Analysis

Kernel density spreads event values across a raster. A small radius keeps local spikes; a large radius emphasizes a broad trend. The course applies it to population change, traffic accidents, and street density.

2D and 3D Population Density Mapping

Equal-area grids, including hexagons, hold the spatial unit constant. 3D extrusion is a visual encoding, not an extra population count.

Practical Applications

  • Urban service planning — density shows concentration; accessibility analysis shows reach.
  • Population distribution assessment — count and density together show concentration vs. spread.
  • Identifying high-density areas — a place to study, not an automatic problem.
  • Urban growth monitoring — density can fall while population rises if boundaries expand.

Conclusion

Population density is people divided by land. The result changes with the spatial unit, the data, and the area measurement. BCR, FAR, kernel density, and 2D/3D grids belong beside the ratio, not in its place.
