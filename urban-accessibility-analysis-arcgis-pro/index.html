GIS-Based Urban Accessibility Analysis Using ArcGIS Pro

Urban GIS Lab · November 7, 2026

A service that looks close on a map may be difficult to reach. Rivers, railways, motorways, and missing pedestrian links sit between households and clinics, schools, or stations. Urban accessibility analysis using GIS measures that separation the way people actually move.

This article explains the ideas. The applied work is in the course Urban Spatial Analysis & Modeling with ArcGIS Pro.

What Is Urban Accessibility?

Accessibility is how readily people can reach opportunities: hospitals, schools, parks, jobs, or transit. It connects origins, destinations, and the cost of movement between them. A count of facilities in a district is not an accessibility measure.

Why Accessibility Matters in Urban Planning

Two neighborhoods can have the same population and very different access. Population density analysis shows where people are concentrated. Accessibility analysis asks whether they can reach what they need. A short trip on the network is also not proof of service quality.

Distance-Based vs Network-Based Accessibility

Euclidean distance = √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²]

Even a correct straight-line distance ignores the street system. It crosses rivers and closed roads as if they were open ground.

Network distance = the minimum sum of link costs along a connected path

Travel time is often the more useful impedance. Connectivity matters as much as length: bridges and overpasses must connect only where a traveler can transfer.

Housing and a clinic 300 m apart across a river, with the only bridge 1.2 km upstream, can require a walk on the order of 2.4 km. The straight line says “close.” The network says otherwise.

A short straight-line distance is not evidence that a service is easy to reach.

GIS Methods for Accessibility Analysis

Euclidean Distance Analysis

Useful as a screen when no network exists. A poor final description of urban access wherever barriers matter.

Network Analysis

A network dataset stores links with distance or time impedance. The course builds networks from local data and OpenStreetMap. Missing paths and wrong one-way rules pass into the result.

Service Area Analysis

The region reachable from a facility within a cutoff (for example 10 minutes or 800 m). Overlaps show redundancy; gaps show places outside the cutoff. The polygon does not know facility capacity.

Nearest Facility Analysis

Finds the closest destinations along the network, with path and cost. The course includes a police-station case relative to a metro station.

Origin-Destination Matrix

Stores travel cost between many origins and destinations. A simple reading is cumulative access: how many destinations fall within a threshold.

Location-Allocation

Chooses candidate sites to serve demand, often maximizing population coverage within a travel cutoff. The course includes maximum-coverage workflows, including an application in Milan.

Practical Urban Applications

  • Healthcare, schools, emergency services, and public facilities
  • Walking networks for education access
  • Transportation planning with OD costs across zones

How ArcGIS Pro Supports This Work

Raster distance tools screen conditions. Network Analyst handles routing, service areas, nearest facilities, OD matrices, and location-allocation. Check connectivity before trusting the map.

Limitations and Data Considerations

  • Missing paths change walking access more than driving access.
  • Impedance assumptions (speed, peak hour) are part of the finding.
  • A neighborhood centroid hides people at the edge.
  • A car network does not describe walking or transit.

Conclusion

Urban accessibility follows ways people can travel. Straight-line distance is a screen. Network methods describe reach when the cutoff, mode, and demand unit are stated clearly.
